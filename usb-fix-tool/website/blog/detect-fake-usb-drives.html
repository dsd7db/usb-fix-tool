How to Detect Fake USB Drives (Free Capacity Test)

A "2 TB" USB stick for $12 is not a bargain — it's a counterfeit. The controller has been reprogrammed to lie about its capacity, and the drive will silently destroy your files the moment you exceed the real flash inside. Here's how the scam works and how to expose it in minutes, for free.

How fake-capacity USB drives work

Every USB flash drive has a small controller chip that tells the operating system how big the drive is. Counterfeiters buy cheap 4–16 GB flash chips, then reflash the controller firmware so it reports 256 GB, 512 GB, 1 TB — whatever the listing promises. Windows believes the controller, shows the fake size in Explorer, and even lets you "copy" hundreds of gigabytes onto the drive.

The deception falls apart in one of two ugly ways:

  1. Wrap-around writes. Once the real flash is full, the controller starts writing new data over the top of your old files, starting from the beginning. The file names all still appear in Explorer — but the contents are gone. You discover it weeks later, when you try to open the photos.
  2. Discarded writes. Cheaper fakes simply throw the extra data away. Copying "succeeds", but reading the file back returns garbage or an I/O error.

Either way, the drive looks perfectly healthy until the exact moment you rely on it. That's why every suspicious drive should be tested before you trust it with real data.

Red flags before you even test

  • Price too good to be true. Legitimate 1 TB flash media costs many times more than the $9–$15 marketplace listings. There is no "factory surplus" of 2 TB sticks.
  • Unknown brand + huge capacity. 512 GB and up from a brand you've never heard of is the classic combo.
  • Suspicious speed. Fake drives are usually built from the slowest flash available. If a "USB 3.2" drive writes at 4 MB/s, be suspicious.
  • Capacity that's exactly a round marketing number. Real drives show slightly less than the label (a 64 GB stick shows ~57.5 GiB). Some fakes report the marketing number exactly.

Red flags are hints. The only proof is a full write-and-verify test.

The only reliable test: write everything, read everything back

A capacity test works like this: the tool fills the drive's free space with files of known, unique test data, forces the data out of the cache and onto the actual flash, then reads every byte back and compares it against what was written. On a genuine drive every byte matches. On a fake, the verification phase finds mismatched data exactly where the real flash ran out — and the test tells you the drive's true usable capacity.

This is the method made famous by the German utility H2testw, and it remains the gold standard because it can't be fooled: the controller can lie to Windows about its size, but it can't fake data it never stored.

Interpreting a FAIL result

Suppose you tested a "512 GB" stick and got: verified 29.7 GB, lost/corrupted 448 GB. That tells you the drive contains roughly 32 GB of real flash. You now have two options:

  • Return it and report the listing. A fake-capacity drive is fraud in most jurisdictions, and marketplaces refund these readily when you include the test result.
  • Shrink it to its real size and keep using it. If a refund isn't possible, you can make the drive safe by re-partitioning it to its true capacity: delete the partition, create a new one no larger than the verified size, and format it. USB Fix Tool's USB Partitions tab does exactly this — it works only on removable USB flash drives, so you can't accidentally touch an internal disk. Re-test afterwards to confirm the new partition passes.

Why not just copy a big folder onto the drive?

Because Windows caches writes and fakes often keep file names intact while destroying file contents. A copy that "succeeds" proves nothing — you'd have to reopen and binary-compare every file to be sure. A proper capacity test does that comparison automatically with unique, per-block test patterns, forces real flushes to the flash (so the cache can't hide anything), and counts every corrupted byte.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a full capacity test take?

Total time ≈ drive size ÷ write speed + drive size ÷ read speed. A genuine 64 GB USB 3 stick writing at 60 MB/s and reading at 120 MB/s takes about 27 minutes. Ironically, fakes often fail much faster maths: they're built from slow flash, so a "1 TB" fake writing at 5 MB/s would take days — one more reason to stop the test and demand a refund once errors appear.

Will the test erase my files?

No. The test writes its own files to free space only and deletes them afterwards. Existing files are never modified. Even so, always back up first — a counterfeit drive can corrupt neighbouring data all by itself.

Is a quick 1 GB test enough?

Only as a first smoke test. Fakes typically contain 8–32 GB of real flash, so small writes land on genuine storage and verify fine. To prove capacity you must fill the drive — that's the point where the controller runs out of real flash and starts corrupting data.

My drive failed the test. Can I get my money back?

Usually yes. Screenshot the FAIL result panel (it shows tested, verified and lost capacity) and open a "item not as described" claim. Marketplaces resolve fake-capacity claims quickly because the evidence is unambiguous.

Can a fake drive be "fixed" to full size?

No. The flash chips physically hold only what they hold — no software can add storage. Re-partitioning to the verified real size makes the drive honest and safe, but it will never hold more than the flash inside it.

Does this work for SD cards and external SSDs too?

Yes — the write-and-verify method works on any storage that mounts with a drive letter or folder. Counterfeit microSD cards are at least as common as fake USB sticks, and external "portable SSDs" with fake capacity have flooded marketplaces since 2023.

Already lost files to a fake drive?

If a counterfeit stick corrupted your photos or documents, dedicated recovery software can sometimes restore what the controller didn't physically overwrite.
